\vspace{0.35em}
\noindent\textbf{A useful robotic material cell should not merely move; it should accept a brief command, enter a legible mechanical state, and hold that state without continuous power. \fluxcell{} frames that capability as pulse-programmed mechanical memory. The present manuscript defines a corrected electropermanent-magnetic Sarrus-cell architecture in which square pole plates remain continuous, solid steel keepers translate through the working gap, and orthogonal X/Y layers create a two-axis cell from the same one-axis proof. The current contribution is deliberately bounded: a figure-grounded geometry lock, a purchase-ready proof, and the exact evidence gate needed before force, efficiency, lifetime, or reliable two-axis operation can be claimed.}

\section{Introduction}

Soft robots and robotic materials increasingly distribute function through body geometry, material state, and embedded physical computation rather than through a single remote actuator \cite{rus2015,wehner2016,byun2024}. This direction is attractive because mechanical structure can store information, route signals, and shape motion before software has to intervene. A central unresolved question is not simply how to actuate a compliant structure, but how to make a repeated unit that changes state, stores that state physically, and tiles into a larger machine without paying continuous electrical power to remember its last command.

Electropermanent magnets offer a direct route to low-standby-power state retention. An \epm{} combines magnetic materials with different coercivities so that a short current pulse can switch the net external field while the magnetized state persists after current returns to zero \cite{knaian2010,johnson2024,zhu2019}. EPMs have been used for programmable matter, modular robotic connection, magnetic soft-robot components, and magnetorheological fluid control \cite{knaian2010,zhu2019,johnson2024,ntella2023}. In parallel, physical-interface work has shown that embedded magnets can create legible, passive haptic states in digitally fabricated mechanisms \cite{zheng2019}.

\fluxcell{} connects those threads to a Sarrus-cell robotic-matter concept. The design does not ask a small magnet to pull a compliant mechanism across a large open gap. Instead, a fixed \epm{} cartridge creates a localized magnetic circuit, while steel fork and tongue keepers slide through continuous pole-plate gap regions as the Sarrus-compatible frame changes width. The first experiment is therefore reduced to a compact overlap problem: pulse the cartridge, record whether geometry changes, check whether the state persists at zero current, reverse the pulse, and measure the limits. This is a stronger scientific object than a broad concept sketch because it can fail cleanly. Either pulse-programmed keeper overlap produces a visible held mechanical state, or the measurement identifies the dominant failure mode.

\section{Results}

\fluxcell{} is presently a pre-measurement design result. The result is therefore not an efficiency claim or a force-density claim. It is the conversion of a speculative robotic-matter idea into a physically ordered architecture, a locked bill of materials, and a small proof whose required traces are defined before the bench test begins.

\subsection{The Y layer defines the one-axis proof}

The one-axis proof is the Y layer of the final two-axis geometry (Fig.~\ref{fig:y-layer}). It uses a lane order of fork, Alnico rod, center tongue, NdFeB rod, and fork. The full colored layer represents the physical magnetic gap region; no slots or white lanes are cut into the pole plates. This correction matters because the magnetic circuit is no longer being described as a set of separated lanes in the steel. The pole plates stay continuous, while the dark steel keepers move over the gap region.

\subsection{The X layer gives the same proof an orthogonal axis}

The X layer is the same geometry rotated 90 degrees (Fig.~\ref{fig:x-layer}). It preserves the 3.0 inch expanded/off and 1.5 inch contracted/on widths while orienting the keeper motion left-to-right. This is the simplest route from the one-axis coupon to the intended two-axis cell: prove one layer, then stack the same layer logic in the orthogonal direction. The figure also defines the packaging boundary. The Alnico and NdFeB positions remain inside the gap region; the moving fork and tongue keepers supply the visible state change.

\subsection{The assembled cell should look simple from the outside}

The opaque assembled view makes the external object testable without exposing every internal lane (Fig.~\ref{fig:opaque}). The top steel pole plate hides the Alnico and NdFeB rods and the internal magnetic gaps. The observer should see the top plate, carriers, and protruding steel keepers. This is important for the physical proof because the visible object should not require the reader to infer hidden geometry from a complicated exploded view. A successful bench specimen should make the same external state change legible on video while the current, temperature, and width traces provide the evidence.

\subsection{The dimensional lock creates a falsifiable mechanical-memory proof}

The current dimensional correction uses 1.00 inch keeper inserts rather than 1-1/8 inch inserts. With 1.25 inch square pole plates, the one-axis proof targets a 3.0 inch expanded/off width and a 1.5 inch contracted/on width, corresponding to a 0.75 inch side stroke on each side. The off-state overlap is approximately 0.125 inch and the on-state overlap is approximately 0.875 inch. These numbers are design targets, not measured performance. Their value is that they make the first physical test falsifiable: a synchronized record can show width, pulse polarity, current, hold at zero drive, release after reverse pulse, and coil temperature.

\subsection{The publishable evidence gate is a synchronized trace}

The manuscript becomes an actuator paper only if the one-axis proof produces a synchronized record rather than a single success photograph. The minimum publishable trace is deliberately small (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}). It has to show the command, the electrical cost of the command, the mechanical response, and whether the state remains after drive current returns to zero. This makes the evidence portable: the same trace can support a robotics claim, a mechanical-memory claim, and a later four-cell surface demonstration.

\section{Discussion}

The strongest version of \fluxcell{} is not a generic magnetic actuator paper. It is a paper about pulse-programmed mechanical memory. The state variable is not merely magnetic polarity; it is a visible mechanical configuration coupled to magnetic overlap and held after the electrical pulse ends. If the one-axis proof records clean switching, zero-current hold, reverse-pulse release, bounded heating, and repeated cycles, the work can move from design protocol to actuator evidence.

The source boundary matters because overclaiming would make the project weaker, not stronger. This manuscript does not claim force density, efficiency, safety, lifetime, or reliable two-axis behavior. It claims that the current design has become concrete enough to test and that the proof has been reduced to a small set of measurements. That reduction is the paper's present contribution. It makes the next experiment difficult to hand-wave: either the geometry moves and holds under a measured pulse, or the data identify the dominant failure mode.

For R\&D in physical experiences, the compelling artifact would be a small surface that changes contour, holds while electrically quiet, and returns on command. That future demonstrator would be human-readable because the motion, latch, and reset are visible. This is the portfolio-facing reason the project matters for an Imagineering-style R\&D path: it combines physical computing, visible mechanism design, soft-robotic structure, rapid fabrication, and evidence-rich iteration in one inspectable artifact. The current paper does not claim that demonstrator exists, nor does it imply any hiring outcome or institutional relationship. It defines the cell-level experiment that must pass before that story is credible.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{One-axis proof geometry}

The proof uses a fixed center EPM cartridge with two 1.25 inch square pole plates made from 0.25 inch by 1.25 inch 1018 steel rectangle bar cut to 1.25 inch length. The magnetic elements are one 0.250 inch diameter by 0.625 inch long Alnico 5 rod and one 1/4 inch diameter by 5/8 inch long NdFeB rod. The moving keepers are two A36 fork prong inserts, each 1/8 inch by 1/2 inch by 1.00 inch, and one 1018 center tongue insert, 1/4 inch by 1/2 inch by 1.00 inch. The Y/X/opaque figures in this draft are cropped from the supplied source PDF without changing figure content \cite{fluxcellGeometry2026}.

\subsection{Electrical pulse protocol}

The Alnico coil is insulated with Kapton, wound with 30 AWG magnet wire, and connected to an H-bridge driver from a current-limited bench supply. The first test uses short bidirectional pulses separated by zero-drive intervals. For each pulse, the record should include polarity, pulse duration, current limit, observed width, hold state after zero drive, release state after reverse pulse, and a coil temperature note.

\subsection{Acceptance boundary}

The first proof succeeds only if the one-axis assembly visibly changes width, holds a state after current returns to zero, releases or reverses under the opposite pulse, and stays within a temperature range safe enough for continued bench testing. A partial result remains useful if it identifies the dominant failure mode: weak magnetic force, high friction, carrier twist, thermal limit, or poor release margin.
